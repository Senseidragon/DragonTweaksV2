# Mangrove Swamp

The roots come first. You're walking along the coast and the ground softens and the trees start growing roots that reach into the water like fingers, and suddenly you're in the mangrove swamp without quite having decided to enter it.

The water here is teal, not the murky gray-green of a regular swamp — cleaner-looking but no clearer once you're underneath. Underwater visibility drops almost immediately. The fog closes in from all directions and you navigate by the root systems above you, filtering down light that turns everything blue-green and strange. Tropical fish dart between the prop roots. Frogs — warm-colored, orange and yellow — hop across the mud and the lily pads with a focused nonchalance that suggests they own the place, which they more or less do.

The mangrove swamp is quieter than the swamp proper. No witch huts. No sculk in the mud. The threats are simpler and less personal: slimes at night when the moon is right, the occasional bogged archer in the undergrowth. It doesn't feel hostile so much as indifferent — the swamp will inconvenience you, but it's not invested in the outcome.

The mud is what it is. You'll sink into it, you'll track it places, and you'll develop opinions about it that I won't try to influence. Practical applications exist — mud bricks are solid, the mangrove wood is distinctive, and the propagules that fall from the branches can be carried and replanted anywhere with water. You can move the mangrove with you, which is more than most biomes offer.

The orange haze sits heavy in the afternoons. It makes everything feel like late summer, even when it isn't.

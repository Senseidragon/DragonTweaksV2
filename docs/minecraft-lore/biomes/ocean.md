# Ocean

The ocean is where the world runs out of land and keeps going anyway.

You sail until the seafloor drops away and the water turns deep blue and the bottom is gravel in the dim light a long way down. Kelp forests reach toward the surface in slow green columns, swaying in currents you can't feel from the boat. Dolphins surface and pace you for a while — they seem to find boats interesting, or amusing, or worth accompanying for reasons that are their own. Squid drift. Fish school and scatter.

Beneath the ordinary surface is the cold. The deeper ocean variants are a different world entirely: the light goes, the color goes, the seafloor becomes stone and the pressure is implied if not mechanical. Ocean ruins sit on the gravel in varying states of dissolution — stone walls and mossy cobblestone, whatever used to be rooms now open to the water, whatever used to be people long since concluded. Shipwrecks rest at angles, masts broken, holds full of maps and cargo and the history of voyages that ended here.

The cliffs near oceanic islands can be extraordinary. A hundred blocks of stone dropping straight into the water, nothing to break the line from summit to surface. From the top, the ocean is a color that doesn't have a name between blue and green, darker at depth, lit from above.

There are ocean monuments, but those deserve their own accounting.

The ocean at night — surface level, in a boat, under stars — is one of the finer things available in this world if you're willing to accept the terms. The terms being: complete exposure, nothing to hide behind, everything possible arriving from any direction. Some people find that peaceful. They're not wrong.

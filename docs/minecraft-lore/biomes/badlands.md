# Badlands

The terracotta tells the story if you know how to read it.

Red, orange, yellow, white, brown — layer after layer banded through the cliffs and mesas like a history written in stone. Whatever pressure and time created these formations left records in the color, and if you dig carefully you can trace the same sequence from one plateau to the next. The badlands were built all at once, by the same process, across an enormous span of land. Something happened here, a long time ago, and the ground remembers it.

No rain falls in the badlands. Dry thunder rolls in sometimes — you hear it, you see the lightning, and nothing comes of it. The storms are all form and no substance. The dead bushes don't bend because there's no wind, not really. Just heat and silence and the creak of the world settling.

The mineshafts are different here. Everywhere else they're buried, hidden, found by accident. In the badlands the mesas have eroded away until the shafts hang exposed in cliffsides — dark oak beams and rusty rails jutting out over a hundred-block drop, the scaffold of someone's old operation left dangling in dry air. Whoever worked these mines is long gone. The gold they left behind isn't.

Gold veins run high in the badlands, higher than they have any right to. Surface level, near surface. Makes no geological sense and I stopped worrying about geological sense some time ago. The armadillos don't worry about it either. They roll up when you get too close and wait for the threat to pass, which is a perfectly reasonable strategy in a place where the real dangers are all underground anyway.

It's beautiful in the way that abandoned things are beautiful. Striking and a little sad and better for not being touched too much.

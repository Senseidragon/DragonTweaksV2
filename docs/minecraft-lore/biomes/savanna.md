# Savanna

No rain.

That's the first thing you notice, after a while — it never rains here. Thunder rolls through sometimes, the sky darkens, lightning reaches down and strikes things, and nothing comes of it. Dry storms. The ground stays dry. The acacia trees keep their flat-topped, windswept silhouettes against the orange sky and nobody gets wet.

The savanna is wide and warm and sparsely populated by trees that look like they've been arguing with the horizon for years. Acacia is a distinctive wood — orange-tinted, slightly curved in the growing, immediately recognizable. The villages here are built of it, mixed with terracotta, and they look exactly like they belong to a place where rain is theoretical. Sturdy, low, sun-baked. The armadillos roll around at the margins, unbothered.

Llamas wander in the higher reaches where the savanna becomes savanna plateau, which is the same biome but vertical — flat-topped mesas with sheer drops and the same sparse acacia on top. Llamas in the flatlands too, occasionally, being llamas about it. Donkeys are common enough that you start expecting them.

The savanna borders plains and forests and deserts, and in each direction the transition is legible — the air gets drier or wetter, the trees change, the color of the grass shifts. The savanna occupies a specific temperature, a specific tolerance for arid conditions, and it knows its own edges.

Pillager outposts appear here. They appear everywhere, but they look more at home in the savanna somehow — the same spare, functional architecture, the same watchtower, the same grim purposefulness. Place them in a flower forest and they look wrong. Here they just look like part of the landscape, which might be the most unsettling thing about them.
